She starts her book “Translated woman : crossing the border with Esperanza story” with
her logic that just like we don’t choose the family, similarly it is not us who choose the
story we write. The introduction of the book “Talking serpent” begins with the Esperanza
belief of talking serpents, where killing it requires cutting out its tongue to prevent it from
turning you into an evil one in the afterlife. While the author was listening to Esperanza's
story, she shared her fear that if other women of her community would read about her
life, they would gossip and laugh at her. So Esperanza requests her to mask the serpent
(here her narrative) by translating it. Speech is not neutral, it has moral and spiritual
consequences and often gets suppressed under the power dynamics within family and
society. This made her raise a critical question- who gets to speak? Who controls the
narrative?
This book is a work of participant observation, where she sits everyday after the dinner
with Esperanza with whom she was related as Comadre (fictive relative). Esperanza is
a single mother and street peddler, which puts her lowest on the social ladder. Her
neighbor considers her as a witch, who made her husband blind when he abused her
and left her for another woman. The author encounters her through the pre-existing
stereotype , which portrayed her as a witch-like, dangerous and socially-deviant woman.
These narratives highlight the marginalisation of women. Constructed through fear and
suspicion, revealing the various social processes that shaped her knowledge even
before the ethnographic interaction begins.
The author was hesitant to approach Esperanza, reflecting a deeper reflexive critique of
anthropology. She was aware of a troubling similarity between anthropology and
historical practices like the inquisition, which forced people to confess their stories,
anthropologists also extract life narrative from their research subjects. This makes her
question whether her role is truly neutral or if it involves a form of control over other’s
voices. As she is a privileged outsider working among poorer rural Mexican people. This
creates an unequal relationship where she has more power- economic, educational and
institutional as compared to esperenza. Because of this imbalance, even a simple act
like asking someone to share their life story can become ethically complicated. This
makes her think if she is genuinely listening or subtly imposing authority by turning
someone’s lived experience into academic knowledge.
Their relationship is transformed into compadrazgo (ritual kinship), when the author
becomes Esperenza's comadre. This bond establishes a framework of reciprocity,
where the comadre is expected to provide material support while receiving access to
Esperenza's life story. Still the relationship is not free of power dynamics. This
transformation serves as a partial decolonization of the ethnographic relationship,
grounding it in local cultural practices with a reminder that structural inequalities cannot
be erased. This relationship is both intimate and unequal, personal and political at the
very same time.
Behar doesn’t asks the details, rather Esperanza decides what to tell and what to not.
She herself controls its pacing, tone and structure. She conceptualizes her life through a
specific narrative structure. Her story is not just about events, it is about transformation
of pain into meaning through storytelling. It suggests that lives marked by struggle are
seen as more meaningful to tell, as if suffering gives a story its depth and importance.
The introduction is a theoretical intervention in anthropology, feminism and postcolonial
studies, demonstrating that storytelling is a site of power, resistance and
meaning-making. She discovers the complexity in writing, which involves transformationin multiple forms - oral to written, spanish experience into english narrative, and she
redefines ethnography as collaborative process, a form of co-authorship. The author is
not just a listener but a mediator and storyteller in her own way.
